A minimum viable product (MVP) exists to answer one question: will people use this? Speed matters more than elegance, so many teams start with no-code tools, templates or a quickly assembled codebase. That is the right call — until it isn't.

## Signs your MVP has reached its limits

- **Every new feature takes longer than the last.** Workarounds pile up and simple changes break unrelated parts of the system.
- **Performance problems appear with real traffic.** Pages slow down, background jobs time out, or costs rise faster than users.
- **Platform limits block the business.** You need an integration, a pricing model or a data export your tools cannot support.
- **Security and compliance questions arrive.** Larger customers ask about data handling, backups and access control.

If two or more of these sound familiar, it is time to plan the next stage.

## Rebuild or evolve?

A full rewrite is tempting but risky: it freezes product development and often takes longer than expected. In most cases we recommend an **incremental approach**:

1. **Map the system.** Document which parts are stable and which cause the most pain.
2. **Stabilise the data layer first.** A well-designed database and API make every later step easier.
3. **Replace the highest-pain module.** Move one area — for example checkout or reporting — to the new architecture while the rest keeps running.
4. **Repeat with measurable goals.** Track speed, error rates and delivery time after each step.

## What "custom architecture" should mean

Custom does not have to mean complicated. A good architecture for a growing startup is usually:

- A typed codebase (for example TypeScript) with automated tests
- A relational database with clear ownership of data
- Managed cloud services instead of hand-maintained servers
- Continuous deployment so small changes ship safely
